Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers in India

Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) recreated on NSE shares, 2005 to 2026

Pramod Athani

pramod.athani@gmail.com · ORCID 0009-0002-0270-7045 · SSRN 13497529 · LinkedIn

October 2026 · data to 30 October 2026

Abstract

Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) showed that buying American stocks with the highest returns over the past three to twelve months and selling those with the lowest earned about 1% a month over the following months. I recreate their thirty-two strategies on shares listed on the National Stock Exchange of India from January 2005 to October 2026, using daily exchange files that include delisted shares, adjusted for splits, bonuses, dividends and symbol changes, and charge full Indian delivery costs. The paper's six-month strategy earns 1.28% a month after costs among the 500 most traded shares (Newey-West t = 3.07) and 1.36% among all shares, against 0.95% in the original. The spread is smaller among the 200 most traded shares (0.85% a month) and among shares with stock futures (0.65%), and it collapsed by 49% in a single month in May 2009. Because India permits only covered short sales, I also report a long-only version, whose extra return mostly reflects market risk, and a version hedged with NIFTY futures, which earned 1.05% a month after costs from 2012. India shows no January reversal and no long-run reversal among large shares, but quarterly results announcements carry part of the profit, as in the original study.

Keywords: momentum, relative strength, India, NSE, transaction costs, short-sale constraints, replication.

1. Introduction

A momentum, or relative-strength, strategy buys shares that have risen most over recent months and sells those that have fallen most. Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) found that such strategies, applied to NYSE and AMEX stocks from 1965 to 1989, earned significant positive returns over holding periods of three to twelve months, that the profits were not explained by systematic risk, that they came mostly from the winners, that they were reversed every January, and that part of them disappeared over the following two years. Momentum has since become one of the most studied regularities in finance.

This page asks whether the same strategies work on Indian shares, after the costs an Indian investor actually pays, and in forms an Indian investor can actually trade. Everything on it can be explored: the specification panel below sets the ranking period J, the holding period K, the one-week skip, the universe, the portfolio, the cost assumptions and the sample, and every figure and table marked “live” is recomputed in the browser from monthly returns. The code, the data pipeline and every result are in the study's repository.

Specification

2. The original study

At the start of each month, Jegadeesh and Titman ranked every stock on its return over the past J months, with J of 3, 6, 9 or 12, and formed ten equally weighted portfolios. The top tenth are the winners, the bottom tenth the losers. A strategy buys the winners, sells the losers and holds the position for K months, again 3, 6, 9 or 12. Because a new position is opened every month and each is held for K months, the strategy always holds K overlapping portfolios and revises one K-th of its weight each month (p. 68). A second set of sixteen strategies waits one week between ranking and buying, to avoid the bid-ask bounce and short-term reversal. Table 1 shows the original results; the best strategy, 12/3, earned 1.31% a month, and the strategy studied in detail, 6/6, 0.95% (p. 69).

Table 1. Fixed. The original results: average monthly returns of the buy, sell and buy-minus-sell portfolios, NYSE and AMEX stocks, January 1965 to December 1989, no skip (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993, Table I, panel A, p. 70). Plain t-statistics in brackets.

3. Data and adaptation to India

Prices. The study uses NSE's daily price files (bhavcopies) from January 1996 to October 2026: 7,628 trading days and 5,723 symbols, including every share that was later delisted. Prices are adjusted for splits, bonuses and cash dividends from NSE's corporate action records, and histories are joined across 1,078 symbol changes. NSE's records of splits and bonuses are complete only from 2006, so earlier ones are inferred from price jumps that match a common ratio and do not reverse; the 1997 to 2004 sample is therefore less reliable. Moves that are undone within days are treated as misprints. The broker data the study first turned to, the Unified Broker Interface, starts only in December 2019, holds only shares listed today and has missing or misdated adjustments for some shares, so it serves as a cross-check.

Universes. “All NSE shares” follows the paper: every company share (funds, rights entitlements and partly paid shares removed) that traded in the last five days before ranking and has a J-month return. “Top 500” keeps the 500 shares with the highest median daily traded value over the previous six months and a price of at least 10 rupees, an investable stand-in for the NIFTY 500. Ranking uses only data up to the close of the last trading day of the month, and positions are bought at that close.

Costs. Every result after costs charges, on the value actually traded each month, securities transaction tax of 0.1% on each side, stamp duty of 0.015% on purchases, exchange and SEBI charges with GST, and market impact of 0.1% per side for the top 500 or 0.3% for all shares. Brokerage is zero, as at discount brokers; the panel lets the reader change impact and brokerage.

Short selling. India allows only covered short sales, and securities lending is thin, so the paper's short leg cannot be held overnight in cash shares. The winners-minus-losers portfolio is therefore a research benchmark; Section 4.6 reports versions that can be traded: winners only, winners hedged with NIFTY 50 futures, and a long-short among shares with stock futures that sells the losers through futures.

Market and risk-free rate. The market return and the 91-day Treasury bill rate come from the Indian factor library of the Indian Institute of Management, Ahmedabad (Agarwalla, Jacob and Varma, 2013), which also supplies an Indian momentum factor for comparison. The NIFTY 500, approximated as its price return plus a twelfth of its dividend yield, is available from 2012.

4. Results

Tables and figures marked (live) respond to the specification panel above. Those marked Fixed always show the 6/6 strategy over 2005 to 2026 (Table 1 is the original study's own result), because they need results that the page does not hold for other choices.

4.1 The chosen strategy

Table 2 scores the strategy chosen in the specification panel; Figure 1 shows how a rupee would have grown in it, beside its winners and losers and the Indian market, and Figure 2 its drawdowns. With the default choice, the paper's 6/6 strategy among the top 500, the spread earns 1.42% a month before costs and 1.28% after, with a Newey-West t-statistic of 3.07; the paper's figure is 0.95%. The worst stretch was the rebound of 2009, when the losers soared and the spread lost 49% in May alone.

Mean returns are monthly; t-statistics use Newey-West standard errors with the usual automatic lag; alpha and beta come from regressing the strategy's return (less the risk-free rate for one-sided portfolios) on the market's excess return.

4.2 Every ranking and holding period

Table 3 repeats the layout of Table 1 for the chosen universe, gap, costs and sample, and Figure 3 maps the chosen portfolio's average return over every J and K. Over 2005 to 2026 all sixty-four winners-minus-losers strategies (two universes, with and without the gap, four J, four K) earn positive returns after costs. As in the paper, longer ranking periods with short holding periods do best, and waiting a week helps a little. Turnover is a little above the paper's: about 16% of each leg is replaced every month, against about 14% in the paper (84.8% over six months). Costs therefore matter most for the shortest periods: the 3/3 strategy among all shares keeps only 0.49% of its 1.00% a month.

4.3 Deciles, size and risk

Table 4 shows all ten deciles of the 6/6 strategy. Returns rise almost steadily from losers to winners, and both extremes hold shares with higher betas than the middle, as in the paper; the losers have the highest beta of all, so the spread has a negative beta. Table 5 repeats the paper's Table III: within each third of the all-shares universe by traded value and by Scholes-Williams beta, the spread stays between 1.45% and 2.17% a month, and its alpha against the market is significant in every group. Unlike the paper, where the abnormal return came from the winners, in India the losers carry a large negative alpha.

4.4 The calendar

The paper's strategy lost about 7% every January, which it linked to the American tax year. India's tax year ends in March, and Figure 4 shows a different calendar: January is positive, March, the last month of the financial year, is strongly positive among all shares, and April and December are the weak months. July is the most reliable month, positive in more than nine years out of ten.

4.5 After the formation month

The paper followed each set of winners and losers for 36 months and found that half of the first year's gain was lost again in the next two years (Table VII, p. 84). Figure 5 repeats this for formation months from 2005. In India the first month after ranking is positive rather than slightly negative, and among the top 500 the cumulative spread keeps rising, to 17.8% after 36 months; among all shares it reaches 14.3% after a year and then stays flat. The paper also found that winners beat losers around earnings announcements in the first months and lost to them a year later (Table IX, p. 88). Table 6 repeats the test with 106,727 quarterly results filed with NSE from 2007 to 2025: the pattern is the same.

Table 6. Fixed. Three-day returns (days −2 to 0) around quarterly results announcements of past 6-month winners minus past losers, by month after ranking, formations from July 2007; plain t-statistics in brackets.

4.6 Portfolios that can be traded in India

Table 7 shows the versions that respect India's rules. Holding the top-500 winners alone earned 1.75% a month after costs, but with a beta of 1.18 its alpha against the Indian market is only 0.25% a month and not significant; against the NIFTY 500 from 2012 it is 0.63% a month. Hedging the winners with a short NIFTY 50 future of equal value removes most of the market risk and earned 1.05% a month after costs from March 2012, with a worst drawdown of 32%. Selling losers through stock futures is possible only among the 52 to 218 shares that have them, and there the spread is small and not significant.

Table 7. Fixed. Portfolios that can be traded in India, 2005 to 2026 (portfolio C from March 2012): average monthly return, Newey-West t, worst drawdown and alpha against the market, before and after costs.

4.7 Robustness and what was tried

Table 8 changes one assumption at a time for the 6/6 strategy. Trading a day later, paying brokerage or charging every share that stopped trading a loss of 30% or 100% in the following month changes little; vanished shares sit mostly among the losers. A borrowing fee on the short leg and a narrower universe matter most. The study ran 192 strategy series in the main grid and about 150 more in checks; the 6/6 strategy was chosen as the lead before any results were seen, and the best cells, such as 12/3, should be read with suspicion. Forty-two of the sixty-four winners-minus-losers cells clear the Bonferroni bound for sixty-four tests (a t-statistic above 3.16) before costs, and twenty after costs. The study's own momentum series has a correlation of 0.75 to 0.83 with the Indian momentum factor of Agarwalla, Jacob and Varma.

4.8 Further tests of the original study

Tables 9 to 14 repeat the other tests of the 1993 paper on the same 2005 to 2026 data. The first follows the paper's January suggestion: if January were a month of reversals, a trader would flip the spread each January. In India it is not. Momentum earns on average +1.6% to +3.2% in January, so flipping it lowers the return and loses in most Januaries, although the evidence for the loss is weak (t between −1.1 and −1.9). The weighted relative strength strategy of Section III is 0.93 to 0.97 correlated with the decile spread (the paper: 0.95), and the lead-lag coefficient has the paper's negative sign. Momentum also survives the paper's flat charge of 0.5% a side. Most of the Indian alpha comes from the loser leg, which the long-only portfolio cannot capture.

5. Conclusion

Momentum is alive in Indian shares, and on paper it is stronger than in the original American study. Three features of the Indian market limit what an investor can take from it. The profit is concentrated outside the largest, most liquid shares; the short side cannot be held as the paper assumes; and the strategy suffers violent reversals when a falling market turns, as in 2009. A long-only portfolio of recent winners, rebalanced monthly in overlapping cohorts, is easy to run and has beaten the NIFTY 500 since 2012, but mostly by taking more market risk; hedging it with index futures keeps a smaller, steadier excess return. The results-announcement evidence suggests, as Jegadeesh and Titman concluded, that prices react slowly to firm-specific news.
